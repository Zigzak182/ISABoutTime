The work these tools support

Short reference notes on the five areas Seed's tools touch. Written for people doing the work, and for explaining it to the people paying for it.

01

Tree Risk Assessment

Risk combines three judgments: likelihood of failure, likelihood of impacting a target, and consequences of that impact. All three are required — a severe defect over nothing is not high risk, and that distinction is the whole point of the framework.

A Level 1 limited visual is a rapid pass over a population, looking for obvious defects. A Level 2 basic assessment is the standard: a systematic 360-degree ground inspection of the tree, root collar and site, with a mallet and probe. Level 3 adds specialized tools — resistance drilling, sonic tomography, aerial inspection, load testing — to answer a specific question Level 2 raised.

Non-negotiable: every rating carries a stated scope and time frame. A rating without one is not defensible, and that is the first thing a competent cross-examination goes after.

Liability vs. Asset walks both published matrices and shows which cell a given combination lands in.

02

Pruning & Structure

ANSI A300 Part 1 is the standard. Every job should name an objective before a saw comes out, and work to one of five types: cleaning, thinning, raising, reduction, restoration.

Three cuts. A removal cut takes a branch to its parent at the branch collar. A reduction cut shortens a stem to a lateral at least one third the diameter of what was removed. A heading cut goes to a stub or undersized lateral, and outside specific nursery and pollarding practice it is a defect.

Dose matters as much as technique: up to about 25% of live foliage on a young vigorous tree, often 10% or less on a mature one, and close to nothing on a tree already in decline.

Highest-value work in the trade: structural pruning of young trees. Ten minutes establishing a dominant leader at year three prevents the codominant failure that needs a crane at year thirty.

03

Diagnosis & Plant Health Care

Work the pattern before naming a culprit. Damage spread uniformly across many unrelated species points to something environmental — herbicide, salt, grade change, drought, a gas leak. Damage confined to one species and progressing over time points to a pathogen or insect.

Most urban tree problems turn out to be abiotic, and most borers and canker fungi are secondary to a stress nobody diagnosed. Treating the borer without correcting the underlying stress buys a season at most.

Excavate the root flare on every decline call. Girdling roots, planting depth and root collar decay are invisible from the canopy and are among the most common actual causes.

IPM order of operations: monitor and identify, set a threshold, then cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical last and most narrowly targeted. Some findings are cosmetic and the correct intervention is none.

04

Species Selection & Installation

Right tree, right place — specified by cultivar. There is a real difference between "a ginkgo" and a named male ginkgo cultivar, and the difference shows up as fruit odor for fifty years if nobody writes it down.

Plant at the correct depth with the root flare visible, correct circling roots at planting, backfill with native soil, and mulch wide and off the trunk. Establishment runs roughly one year per inch of caliper — that is the irrigation obligation, and it should be stated plainly to whoever is responsible for watering.

Diversity is risk management. The 10-20-30 guideline — no more than 10% of a population in one species, 20% one genus, 30% one family — exists because Dutch elm disease and emerald ash borer erased entire municipal canopies planted as monocultures.

Planting Guide by ZIP applies these filters against 79 taxa and names the cultivars.

05

Construction Tree Protection

Construction kills more established trees than any pest, and it kills them slowly enough that nobody connects the two. Damage arrives three ways: roots severed or crushed, soil compacted by traffic and storage, and grade changed over the root zone. The decline typically shows up on a three-to-seven-year lag, long after the contractor's warranty ended.

The critical root zone is commonly estimated at a radius of 1 ft per inch of trunk diameter, with protection zones specified at 1 to 1.5 ft of radius per inch of DBH. Fence it before equipment arrives — a fence installed after the first excavator pass is decoration. Nothing inside: no traffic, storage, spoil, washout or temporary structures.

Where utilities must cross a root zone, bore beneath rather than trench through. That single decision often determines whether a mature tree survives the project. Where roots must be cut, cut them cleanly with a saw or air excavation tool at the fence line, not torn with a bucket at the trunk.

Write it into the contract with a dollar penalty. Trees preserved by handshake do not survive.

Practices worth refusing

These come up constantly, usually requested by name. Each one makes the tree more hazardous and the next visit more expensive.

  • Topping. Removes the canopy that feeds the tree, opens wounds too large to compartmentalize, and releases a dense mass of weakly attached epicormic sprouts. The result is a more hazardous, shorter-lived tree at higher long-term cost. Reduction cuts to adequate laterals are the legitimate alternative.
  • Lion's tailing. Stripping interior foliage and leaving tufts at the tips moves weight outward, increases wind loading, exposes bark to sunscald, and prevents proper taper development.
  • Cabling a tree that should come down. Hardware supplements sound structure. On a structurally failing tree it transfers liability without reducing risk — and it creates a permanent inspection obligation for the owner.
  • Fertilizer as a diagnosis. It does nothing for compaction, girdling roots, planting depth or borers, which is most of what generates the call.
  • Flush cuts and wound dressings. A flush cut removes the branch collar and destroys the tree's ability to form a barrier zone. Dressings neither speed closure nor prevent decay; the one accepted use is sealing fresh oak wounds in oak wilt regions to deter beetle vectors.
